## 1. Draw Your Roadmap

First, fully understand your research topic and draw a roadmap in your mind — establishing clear start and end points is a great beginning.

## 2. Build the Framework

Have a rough outline of your paper's structure, such as the IMRaD format (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion). Think through what content each section should contain. This also provides positive feedback to your research path, acting as an anchor.

## 3. Define Your Reader

Give your paper an assumed reader. Without a target audience, the paper will feel inconsistent — sometimes too advanced, sometimes too basic.

The virtual reader's level should be: **a PhD student in your field + an expert outside your specific subfield of methodology**. Moreover, different sections have different audiences — the introduction, for instance, should treat the reader as a newcomer to your specific topic.

## 4. The Three-Layer Organization

The biggest struggle for undergraduates and novices is determining whether a passage is too basic or trivial for the reader. Organize your language into three tiers:

1. **Textbook principles** — foundational knowledge; mention briefly
2. **Citation principles** — standard practices in the field; keep concise
3. **Innovation principles** — core derivations, hypotheses, or anomalies; elaborate in detail

## 5. The Vomit Draft Strategy

If you still can't decide whether a point should be included, the best practice for novices is the **"vomit draft strategy"**: first write an extremely detailed, messy draft based on your framework, then gradually trim it down.

When drafting, don't stop to agonize over uncertain points — just write them in (like stream-of-consciousness writing), but mark them with special symbols. For example:

> "This method uses gradient descent (should I explain what gradient descent is here?) to optimize the parameters."

> "The data distribution is shown in Figure 2 (my physical intuition behind this phenomenon is a bit wordy, delete later)."

This keeps your creative flow uninterrupted. Once the draft is complete, bring these questions to your advisor or senior lab mates — they'll tell you precisely which bracketed content stays and which goes. **Use your advisor's experience to substitute for your missing experience**, rather than guessing blindly.

## 6. English Writing Workflow

Most of us are accustomed to writing in our native language first. The optimal workflow is: **write in your native language, then translate into English**. Keep in mind:

1. AI translation may alter your writing style
2. AI-assisted generation should be acknowledged in the paper

The methodology:

- **a. Simplify the source** — remove colloquialisms and redundant modifiers; keep subject-verb-object structures clear
- **b. Back-translation check** — after translation, feed it to a fresh AI context window for back-translation to catch meaning deviations
- **c. Prompt engineering** — don't just say "translate this." Instead:

> *"Rewrite this translation in the style of a Nature/Science paper, using concise academic vocabulary, varying sentence structures, and avoiding cliché phrases."*

Finally, ask a fresh AI in English: **"Where does it sound awkward?"** At this point, your paper is as good as done.
