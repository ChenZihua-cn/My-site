## Writing Fluency

Maintain a clear thinking chain throughout your paper. The introduction is particularly important — it's the reader's first impression of your work.

## Syntax & Diction

- Use simple sentences while avoiding overly long ones
- Use `a`, `an`, and `the` properly
- Correctly use `such as`, `e.g.`, `for example`, `for instance`, and `etc.` (use "etc." sparingly)
- Keep your writing concise and clear
- Avoid repeated sentence structures entirely

## Abbreviation Guidelines

- Define symbols and abbreviations at their first appearance
- Avoid using too many abbreviations
- No more than 3 abbreviations in the abstract
- Use fewer than 5 letters in abbreviations when possible

### Naming Example

- **Bad:** Alternating Direction Method Based Non-negative Latent Factor → ADMNLF
- **Good:** Alternativing-direction-method-based Non-negative Latent Factor → ANLF

> A good abbreviation extracts the initial letters of core concept words, not mechanically every word.

## Figures & Diagrams

When presenting a complex diagram, you must include at least some light textual explanation. Don't leave readers to guess.

## Tense

The recommended default is the **present tense**, unless another tense is necessary. Present tense makes the paper feel more immediate and readable.

## Voice

**Active voice over passive voice.** Active constructions are more direct, more forceful, and easier to read.

## Learn from Great Papers

Copy and adapt good sentence structures from well-written papers in your field. This is one of the fastest ways to improve.

## Contractions

Prefer `cannot` over `can't`, `isn't`, `won't`, `haven't`, `it's`, etc. Academic writing should maintain a formal register.

## Compound Adjectives

Hyphenate compound adjectives between words (the recommended adjective formation). Examples: `state-of-the-art`, `end-to-end`.

## References

Use alphabetically ordered reference lists (e.g., Harvard citation style):

> `[Last name, et al., Year] Paper title, Conference name, Page #s...`

## that vs which

- **that** — introduces a restrictive clause (essential to the meaning)
- **which** — introduces a non-restrictive clause (adds supplementary information)

### Examples

> The method **that** we proposed outperforms baselines.  
> *(restrictive: specifies which method)*

> The proposed method, **which** is computationally efficient, outperforms baselines.  
> *(non-restrictive: adds extra info about the method)*

## Bonus Tips

- Recommend yourself as a reviewer for a journal or conference in your area
- Once you accept a review task, do it well — reviewing is one of the best ways to sharpen your own writing judgment
